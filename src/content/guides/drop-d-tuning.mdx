# Drop D Tuning: How and Why

Drop D is the easiest alternate tuning there is: from standard tuning, exactly one string
moves. The low E string drops a whole step to D, and the other five stay where they are —
**D-A-D-G-B-E** from low to high. It takes about fifteen seconds with a tuner, it's just as
quick to undo, and it unlocks a sound that shows up everywhere from folk fingerpicking to
the heaviest ends of metal.

## How to get there

Start from standard tuning — if your guitar isn't there yet, tune it first (the
[Standard Tuning Reference](/guides/standard-tuning-reference) has every target note and
frequency). Then:

1. Open [the chromatic tuner](/) and play your low E string (E2, 82.41 Hz).
2. Turn the low E's tuning peg to *lower* the pitch. You're heading down a whole step —
   two semitones — to **D2 at 73.42 Hz**.
3. Watch the tuner as you go: it will read increasingly flat against E, then re-label the
   pitch as D and start counting cents against that. Keep going until the display shows
   **D** and settles into the Tuned band.
4. Strum all six strings once and re-check the other five. They almost never need
   adjustment for a one-string change this small, but a ten-second check is cheaper than a
   sour chord.

A useful ear check when you have no tuner: the dropped string should sound exactly one
octave below your open D string (D3, 146.83 Hz). Play them together — an octave rings
clean and beat-free, and even a slightly-off octave is easy to hear.

## What it's for

**One-finger power chords.** In standard tuning, a power chord on the low strings takes two
or three fingers across two frets. In Drop D, the bottom three strings — D, A, D — *are* a
power chord when strummed open, and barring any single fret across those three strings
moves that chord anywhere on the neck. That one change makes fast, chugging riffs and
quick chord shifts dramatically easier, which is why the tuning is a fixture of hard rock
and metal.

**A deeper low end.** The extra whole step of range adds weight to everything, and a low D
pedal tone under open-position chords is a classic songwriting device — D major and D
minor shapes suddenly have a huge root note underneath them.

**Drone-friendly folk and fingerstyle.** Long before metal claimed it, Drop D was a folk
and country-blues tuning: the low D drones under melodies played on the upper strings,
giving a solo guitar a fuller, self-accompanied sound.

Songs across genres lean on it — grunge and alternative rock used it constantly, modern
metal takes it as a starting point, and acoustic players from the fingerstyle tradition
reach for it whenever a piece sits in D.

## What changes on the fretboard

Only the lowest string's map moves, but everything on that string shifts up two frets:
the F that lived at the 1st fret is now at the 3rd, and the G at the 3rd fret is now at
the 5th. Chord shapes that use the low E string need adjusting — an open E major shape no
longer works as written, while D-rooted shapes gain a usable open bass note. The other
five strings are untouched, so scales, barre chords rooted on the A string, and everything
else you know still works.

## One caveat: let it settle

A string that just dropped a whole step is under noticeably less tension, and it doesn't
finish stretching the moment the needle says D. Play for a minute and the low string will
often drift slightly — the string is settling into the new tension, and on many guitars
the reduced pull also lets the neck relax a hair, nudging the other strings. The fix is
routine: after a big detune, play briefly, then **re-check the whole guitar against the
tuner** and touch up anything that moved. Second passes take seconds; fighting a
mysteriously sour low string mid-song takes longer.

When you're done, tuning back up is the same trip in reverse — raise the low string until
the tuner re-labels it E and centers. If Drop D whets your appetite for deeper retunings,
the next step down that road is [DADGAD](/guides/dadgad-tuning), where three strings move
and the whole character of the instrument changes.
