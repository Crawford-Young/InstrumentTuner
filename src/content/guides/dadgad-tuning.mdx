# DADGAD Tuning: The Dsus4 Wash

DADGAD is the modal tuning — the one players reach for when they want the guitar to stop
sounding like chords on a grid and start sounding like an open, ringing wash. Strummed
with no fingers down, it produces a **Dsus4** chord: neither major nor minor, unresolved,
and endlessly reusable as a drone. Three strings move from standard tuning, all of them
down, which makes it an easy trip there and back.

## How to tune it

From standard tuning (E-A-D-G-B-E), three strings drop a whole step and three stay put:

| String | Standard | DADGAD | Change |
| ------ | -------- | ------ | ------ |
| 6th (lowest) | E2 · 82.41 Hz | D2 · 73.42 Hz | down a whole step |
| 5th | A2 · 110 Hz | A2 · 110 Hz | unchanged |
| 4th | D3 · 146.83 Hz | D3 · 146.83 Hz | unchanged |
| 3rd | G3 · 196 Hz | G3 · 196 Hz | unchanged |
| 2nd | B3 · 246.94 Hz | A3 · 220 Hz | down a whole step |
| 1st (highest) | E4 · 329.63 Hz | D4 · 293.66 Hz | down a whole step |

With [the chromatic tuner](/) open, take each moving string down slowly: the display will
read flat against the old note, re-label the pitch as the new one, then count cents
against the new target — stop when it centers in the Tuned band. If you overshoot, drop a
little below and come back **up** to pitch, which keeps the string from slipping flat
afterward. The middle strings rarely move during all this, but a full six-string check at
the end costs ten seconds.

Two built-in ear checks: the outer D strings sit exactly one and two octaves below the
first string's D4, and octaves ring beat-free when true. The 5th string A and the 2nd
string A are an octave pair too.

## Why it sounds the way it does

The open strings spell D-A-D-G-A-D: roots, fifths, and a single fourth (the G). There's no
third anywhere — and the third is the note that commits a chord to major or minor. That's
why the open strum is a suspended chord, and why almost anything you play over it sounds
consonant: melodies on the top strings float over open drones without clashing against a
committed harmony. Fretting just one or two strings while the rest ring open produces big,
harp-like voicings that would take four fingers in standard tuning.

## Who uses it

DADGAD's modern lineage runs through the British and Irish folk revival — it became the
default guitar tuning for **Celtic music**, where its drones mimic the pipes and its
suspended openness fits modal tunes that never resolve to a simple major or minor. From
there it spread to acoustic **fingerstyle** composers, who use it for the same reasons:
self-accompanying bass drones, ringing open strings, and melody lines that sit comfortably
on the re-tuned top strings. It has a rock pedigree too — most famously in heavy,
riff-based acoustic-electric writing where the low D power and the modal ambiguity both
pull their weight.

If you play with a capo, DADGAD keeps working: the intervals travel with the capo, so the
same shapes give you the same wash in any key.

## The caveat: your chord shapes don't transfer

Drop D changes one string; DADGAD changes three, including two of the strings your chord
vocabulary leans on hardest. **Standard chord shapes don't survive the trip.** An open E
shape, a C shape, a barred A-minor — all of them land on wrong notes here. Treat DADGAD as
a small new instrument: learn a handful of its own shapes (they're mostly one- and
two-finger), lean on the open strings, and let the tuning do the harmonic work. Players
who try to translate their standard-tuning vocabulary chord-for-chord usually bounce off;
players who start from drones and melody usually get something musical in the first
sitting.

Coming back to standard is the reverse trip — three strings up a whole step, and the
[Standard Tuning Reference](/guides/standard-tuning-reference) has the exact targets. If
you want a gentler introduction to retuning first, start with
[Drop D](/guides/drop-d-tuning), which is DADGAD's first move and nothing else.
