# Metronome Basics: BPM, Time Signatures, and Tap Tempo

A metronome does one thing — click at a steady rate — and that one thing is the most
reliable practice tool ever invented. This guide covers what the numbers mean, what the
time signature setting changes, and how to actually practice with the click, using
[the metronome on this site](/) as the working example.

## What BPM measures

**BPM is beats per minute**: at 60 BPM there's one click every second; at 120 BPM, two per
second. This site's metronome runs from **40 to 240 BPM** and starts at 120, which covers
everything from a funeral-slow largo to tempos faster than almost any music asks for. Some
useful landmarks: 60–76 BPM is ballad territory, 90–120 covers most pop and rock, and
150+ is where fast punk, bluegrass, and bebop live.

The number describes the *beat*, not the notes. A piece full of fast sixteenth-notes at
80 BPM can feel busier than plain quarter-notes at 160 — BPM tells you how fast the pulse
is, and the rhythm you play subdivides that pulse.

## Time signatures: what 2/4, 3/4, and 4/4 mean

A time signature groups beats into repeating measures, and the metronome marks the
grouping by **accenting the first beat** — you'll hear a higher click on beat one, then
lower clicks for the rest of the measure. This metronome offers **2/4, 3/4, and 4/4**:

- **4/4** — four beats per measure: ONE-two-three-four. The default of pop, rock, blues,
  and most of everything. If you don't know a song's meter, guess 4/4 first.
- **3/4** — three beats per measure: ONE-two-three. Waltzes, many folk songs and hymns,
  and a good number of ballads.
- **2/4** — two beats per measure: ONE-two. Marches and polkas, and a natural fit for
  fast tunes that feel "in two" rather than in four.

Where does something like 6/8 fit? Conceptually it's a *compound* meter: six fast eighth
notes grouped as two big beats of three — ONE-two-three-FOUR-five-six. You can approximate
its feel by setting 2/4 and hearing each click as the start of a three-note group. The
point of any of these settings is the same: the accent tells your ear where the measure
starts, so you always know where "one" is.

## Subdivision: the actual skill

Playing "with a metronome" doesn't mean hitting the clicks — it means keeping your
internal grid aligned with them, including all the space *between* clicks. That's
subdivision, and it's what separates tight timing from lucky timing. Two classic
exercises:

- **Halve the tempo.** If you practice a piece at 120 BPM, set the metronome to 60 and
  hear each click as beat one and beat three — you now have half the external help and
  must fill the gaps yourself. Keep halving and the metronome becomes a periodic
  spot-check on a pulse *you* are generating.
- **Displace the click.** Hear the click as the off-beat ("and" of each beat) instead of
  the beat. It's disorienting at first and enormously effective: you can't lean on the
  click anymore, you have to place it.

## Tap tempo

Tap tempo answers the question "what tempo is this song?" Instead of guessing a number,
you tap the beat and the metronome measures you. On this site's metronome, hit the **Tap**
button in time with the music: it needs at least two taps to compute anything, averages
your most recent taps (up to five), and ignores any tap more than two seconds old — so if
you pause and start tapping again, it cleanly starts a fresh measurement. A few steady
taps get you within a BPM or two of the real tempo, and the result lands directly on the
slider, clamped to the 40–240 range.

## Slow practice: the method

The highest-value way to use all of the above:

1. Find the passage's target tempo (tap it from a recording, or read it off the chart).
2. Set the metronome to **60–70% of that** — yes, painfully slow.
3. Play the passage cleanly several times in a row. Clean means every note placed, not
   merely survived.
4. Raise the tempo a few BPM and repeat. Small increments; the slider's whole point is
   that 4 BPM at a time is available.
5. Stop for the day *before* the tempo where it falls apart.

Slow practice works because it gives your hands time to learn the right motions instead
of rehearsing panic. The metronome keeps you honest — without it, "slow" drifts faster
every repeat.

Once your timing is solid, you can put it to the test: the
[Metronome Duel](/guides/metronome-duel-guide) mode on this site turns tempo-matching into
a two-player game, and it is exactly as humbling as it sounds.
