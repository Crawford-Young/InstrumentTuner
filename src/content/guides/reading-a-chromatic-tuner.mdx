# Reading a Chromatic Tuner: What Cents Mean

A chromatic tuner tells you two things: which note you're closest to, and how far you are
from it. That second number is measured in **cents**, and understanding it is the
difference between chasing the needle around and tuning quickly. This guide explains what
the cents readout actually measures and how [this site's tuner](/) displays it.

## What a cent is

A cent is one hundredth of a semitone — the distance between two adjacent frets on a
guitar, split into 100 equal steps. An octave contains 12 semitones, so it spans 1,200
cents. The scale is **logarithmic**: each cent multiplies the frequency by the same tiny
ratio rather than adding a fixed number of hertz. That design matches how human hearing
works — we perceive pitch by ratios, not by raw frequency differences. A note 10 cents
sharp sounds equally out of tune whether it's a low bass note or a high one.

## Why the needle is not a Hz readout

Because cents are ratios, the same cents error corresponds to very different frequency
errors at different pitches. Take the two E strings on a guitar, both drawn from standard
tuning: low E is E2 at 82.41 Hz, and high E is E4 at 329.63 Hz — two octaves apart. Being
5 cents sharp on the low E means being about **0.24 Hz** high. Being 5 cents sharp on the
high E means being about **0.95 Hz** high — four times the frequency error, but the *same*
needle position and the same perceived out-of-tuneness.

This is why a good tuner displays cents rather than hertz: one scale that means the same
thing everywhere on the instrument. If a tuner showed "+1 Hz," you'd have no idea whether
that was nearly perfect (on a low string) or badly sharp (on a high one).

## How this tuner displays it

The tuner on this site compares the pitch it detects against the nearest note in equal
temperament, referenced to A4 = 440 Hz, and shows the difference on a needle:

- **The needle spans ±50 cents.** Fifty cents is half a semitone — the point at which
  you're closer to the *next* note than the one displayed. Any error larger than that
  clamps to the end of the scale, because past that point the tuner re-labels your pitch
  as the neighboring note instead.
- **The "Tuned" band is ±5 cents.** When the detected pitch is within 5 cents of the
  target, the display reads **Tuned**. Outside that band it tells you the direction to
  move: **Tune up** when you're flat (negative cents), **Tune down** when you're sharp
  (positive cents).

Why ±5 cents and not zero? A vibrating string isn't a laboratory oscillator — its pitch
wavers slightly as the note decays, and the attack of a pluck reads sharper than the
sustain. Most listeners can't reliably hear errors under about 5 cents in normal playing,
and chasing the last cent means fighting noise in both the string and the measurement.
Landing inside the band is in tune for any practical purpose.

## Sharp, flat, and the tune-up habit

**Flat** means below the target pitch (needle left, negative cents); **sharp** means above
it (needle right, positive cents). When you're flat, tighten the string toward the pitch.
When you're sharp, don't just nudge it down to the line: drop *below* the target
deliberately, then come back up to it. Approaching every string from below keeps the
string, nut, and tuning gears loaded in one direction, so the pitch doesn't slip the
moment you start playing. It's the single habit that most improves tuning stability.

Let the note ring while you read the needle — pluck once, watch the sustain, and ignore
the first instant of the attack. Repeated hard picking while turning the peg gives the
tuner (and your ear) a moving target.
